Arming the Rebels: From Montreal to the Death Star and Back

VSpot Media presents. These are the ramblings of an e-commerce consultant trying to be something he is not. Clearly.

Cold open · the stories are mine; the echoes are everywhere

I’ve been rewatching Silicon Valley and looking at the crossovers: the bits that turned up in real life, the bits that were already in other films, the places where I find a little truth and a little solace. Kevin Kelly. 1984. The feeling that we have been here before, usually with worse hair and a better soundtrack. Somewhere in all of it there has to be room for a joke.

At first I wondered if there was one movie in this. There isn’t. There are loads of them: scenes, scripts, half-remembered intros, the best bits of the ’80s, ’90s and 2000s. The 2010s were a bit meh. The early 2020s arrived so clean and polished they looked like they’d been wiped down before we were allowed to touch them. I want the collage: a little Grange Hill, a little school-film humiliation, a little late-night sketch show. Maybe the UK version of Saturday Night Live, because it’s better than the US. Yeah, there, I said it.

I have N.E.R.D. tattooed on my back twice, and In Search Of on my arm. Most people know one of those; only one person knows all three. N.E.R.D. is Pharrell Williams’s band, and the letters stand for “No One Ever Really Dies.” I believe we carry some kind of spiritual energy onward: what we do here changes how we find ourselves somewhere else, and how other people find us, too. That may sound like an odd key for an essay about commerce software. It is. It is also the key I have.

These are an amalgamation of my stories, musings and ramblings. The reporting and named facts have sources; the interpretations, memories and questions belong to me. I’m looking for the movie in the business, and the human beings still standing in the frame.

The Good Will Hunting moment

INT. SCHOOL ASSEMBLY HALL — MORNING

A school hall. A trestle table. A banner reads: THE FUTURE OF COMMERCE STARTS HERE. Three adults sit behind it: a founder, a software salesperson and a consultant. At the back, a shopkeeper is trying to get the projector to recognise a laptop.

The blackboard is covered in chalk equations. In the corner, a question: WHAT MAKES A BUSINESS ENTERPRISE?

CHAIR: We can start with online revenue.
SALES REP ONE: Fifteen to a hundred and twenty-five million.
SALES REP TWO: Up to fifty million, really.

A shopkeeper who has said nothing walks to the board and circles the word BUSINESS.

SHOPKEEPER: How many people have to agree before we can change one product?
FOUNDER: Four price books. Three territories. An ERP that thinks yesterday is tomorrow.

The shopkeeper writes beneath the question: COMPLEXITY. A beat. The room gets quiet. The scene borrows the pleasure of a hidden mind solving the problem everyone else was measuring around.

The bell rings. CUT TO TITLE.

ARMING THE REBELS

Bridge out of the scene: I keep coming back to the mismatch between the label on the door and the work happening inside the room. We have been calling this a technology market. It may be a school story: who gets a seat, who writes the rules, and what happens when the kids at the back inherit the timetable.

The argument under the joke

The nerds won the school. Then they built the timetable, the marketplace, the loyalty system, the performance review and the machine that predicts how everyone feels about it.

The thing I keep coming back to: Silicon Valley’s rebel myth helped sell a future of liberation through technology. In commerce, that promise became a fragmented stack of platforms, SaaS tiers, agencies, brands and merchants whose interests do not neatly align. Now AI agents promise to act inside that stack, while the same systems increasingly shape work, attention and human feeling. The satire comes from watching the underdogs become administrators.

The nerds have always won. It has been true since the dawn of time—in every movie and every television show. What seems absurd to me has always been funny, and it runs through all my writing. The Revenge of the Nerds was my working title. If you know the movie, you know what I mean: nostalgic, knowingly blunt, and a reminder that all of us get a shot, whoever we are.

The Montreal-to-Death-Star route is my story’s map. Shopify itself began in Ottawa: its founders built an online snowboard shop, then turned the software they needed into a platform. That origin makes the rebel story sharper, not less useful. Shopify’s account of its founders’ first store and its own history of its Ottawa headquarters.

Three-season plot arc

From rebels to governors

SEASON ONE

Arm the Rebels

“I’m not even supposed to be here today!” · Clerks

The small merchant gets the tools once reserved for large retailers. The pitch is liberation: launch a store, find your people, own the relationship. SaaS pricing tiers look like a school admissions ladder—Free, Freemium, Paid, Plus, Enterprise—while everyone quietly hopes to graduate to the top table.

That is why I keep coming back to Shopify. It does not have to become Salesforce or Adobe to matter. Its story is arming the rebels: make commerce tools available to a much wider pool, then keep widening it. The harder question is what keeps the people human as that platform grows.

I have written around that same promise in The Seduction of Efficiency / Agentism and in my notes on cultural memory and the stories we inherit. The old tune comes back wearing new software.
SEASON TWO

Enterprise Is a Feeling

“Show me the money!” · Jerry Maguire

“Enterprise” becomes a costume passed between the brand and the vendor. The brand imagines itself as a global operation; the vendor sees a revenue band and a larger contract. SaaS companies build for the large account because it looks like the final level, and because a story about enterprise readiness can sound like a story about the company being ready to sell. But enterprise does not mean merely big. It means many people running many processes at once, with real consequences when they collide. Then the real business arrives: regions, price books, stock, people, integrations, exceptions, and twenty systems with different ideas about what “available” means.

Cold open

Escalation

One black legging becomes six colours, three territories and a warehouse full of regret.

Turn

A conference stage promises transformation; the useful conversation happens by the loading bay.

Finale

The agent sees the stock, the ad, the velocity and the missed sale. It asks what the business wants to do.

History repeats — cue Shirley Bassey. Retail has always made its living by knowing what belongs where, when, and for whom. We keep rediscovering assortment, local knowledge and margin as if a dashboard invented them. This is the old craft under a new interface; I have circled the human side of that problem in The View From the Front Row.
SEASON THREE

The New Internet Has a Guidance Counsellor

“I need some guidance, Counselor.” · The Simpsons

The decentralized internet promised escape from the old gatekeepers. AI agents promise to do the clicking, comparing, buying and managing for us. But who sets their goals? Which products do they show? Which workers do they monitor? Which feelings become a risk score, a service prompt, a productivity signal or a policy problem?

History repeats — cue Shirley Bassey. The machine that promised to connect us grows a hand on the tiller: what we see, what we buy, how work is measured, which feelings count as useful signals. I have been following that question in Vinland, and in The Seduction of Efficiency / Agentism. We have seen this before again; the tools and the people subject to them keep changing.

Scene 01 · The rebel promise

Everyone gets a shop. Everyone gets a chance.

The small merchant can now get tools once associated with much larger retailers. The story to tell is both true and incomplete: access widened; durable demand, customer acquisition and margin did not arrive in the box.

Evidence to work from: Shopify’s own annual reporting and merchant/GMV disclosures; cite the exact year and metric in the finished essay. Shopify investor reporting. For the Silicon Valley rebel myth and the show’s tech advisers, see DGA Quarterly.

My story space: A merchant who launched, found an audience, and learned what it costs to keep that audience.

Scene 02 · The closed DTC door

The plan was to build a brand, then sell the brand.

For a generation of operators, the hoped-for exit became part of the pitch: community, paid acquisition, email, repeat orders, then a buyer. What did the real economics ask of the founder after the launch party?

Evidence to add: sourced acquisition-cost and DTC profitability cases, with company names and dates. Don’t use “the market is closed” as a universal fact; make it the reported experience of the people in the story.

My story space: One founder’s long view, including the moment the hoped-for sale stopped being the plan.

Scene 03 · Enterprise, according to whom?

Two salespeople. Two revenue definitions. One complicated business.

Use the remembered $15m–$125m and “up to $50m” definitions as an explicitly personal anecdote. The point is not to claim a formal definition; it is to show how the label shifts with the person selling it.

Context: enterprise complexity often comes from processes, entities, integrations, roles and exceptions rather than a single online-revenue threshold. Use the brand’s actual operating map when telling the scene.

My story space: A real conversation that shows what the revenue shorthand left out.

Scene 04 · The enterprise mirage

Big is a number. Enterprise is a Monday morning.

There is a SaaS version of the same mismatch. We say “enterprise” because it sounds like the top rung, the big logo, the eventual exit. Yet supporting a large, fragmented business is about people, processes, exceptions, access, risk and coordination, not just a higher monthly invoice.

My working question: Are we designing for the actual operating complexity, or for the customer we imagine will make the company look acquirable? That is a question for the essay, not a claim about any one vendor’s motive.

My story space: A software team, a sales promise, and the first time the customer’s real workflow arrived in the room.

Scene 05 · The same product everywhere

One item, six tabs, a race to the lowest visible price.

Product ubiquity makes price comparison easy. The counter-move is not cosmetic price discrimination: it is better sourcing, territory-aware assortment, local relevance, bundles and a clear reason for the product to be there.

Research anchors: use matched-product price studies and local CSO/Eurostat retail data for the precise markets and dates. Eurostat digital economy data.

My story space: A product decision where the business earned margin by changing what it offered, not just changing the number on the tag.

Scene 06 · The keynote and the loading bay

The future was on stage. The problem was by the coffee urn.

Keep the event human. In-person selling matters because listening can expose the real job beneath the category label. The satire lands when every week needs a shiny event recap but the merchant still cannot get an answer about a return, a stock exception or an integration.

Related writing: The View From the Front Row — on gathering, listening and finding the conversation underneath the spectacle.

My story space: One useful conversation after the stage lights went out.

Season finale

Who gets to set the rules?

The people who once wanted a seat at the table now build the table, the invitation list and the system that decides who is ready to sit down. I still believe tools can help people understand their own lives. I also know that the people inside the system do not get to choose every rule it learns.

That is where the films meet: the corridor, the boardroom, the screen full of agents waiting for an objective. I am not sure whether this is a revenge story, a reunion, or the bit just before the next bell. I know there is room for a laugh, and there is room for a person. This is a story from VSpot Media.
